Part 1: The Mystery Crate

Find the mass using Newton's second law. ≈ 45 min

The story

A shipping company lost the labels on three crates. There's no scale big enough, but they do have a force sensor, a photogate timer, and a long smooth floor. Your job is to find your crate's mass using physics.

The plan

Push the crate with a known force and time how long it takes to travel 4.00 m from rest. Then:

acceleration: a = 2d ÷ t²
net force: Fnet = Fa − Ff

Repeat with 5 different forces and graph Fnet (y) vs. a (x). Since ΣF = m·a, the graph is a straight line, and its slope is the mass.

Where does a = 2d ÷ t² come from?

For an object that starts from rest with constant acceleration, d = ½·a·t². Solve for a: a = 2d ÷ t².

Step 1 · Draw the FBD first

In your Lab Doc (section 1A), draw the FBD of the crate while it slides. There are 4 forces. Check yourself against the live FBD in the simulation after you've drawn yours.

Step 2 · Collect data

  1. Pick your crate letter under the simulation. Write the friction value in your Lab Doc.
  2. Set the applied force to 40 N with the − / + buttons, then press ▶ Run.
  3. Record the stopwatch time when the crate crosses the photogate (also listed in the run log).
  4. Repeat for 60, 80, 100, and 120 N.
  5. Try 20 N once, just to see what happens. (You'll need it for Question 5.)

Step 3 · Calculate

For each trial, find a and Fnet. Here's a worked example with practice numbers (a different crate, not yours):

GivenFa = 50 N, Ff = 20 N, d = 4.00 m, t = 2.53 s a = 2d ÷ t²2(4.00) ÷ (2.53)² = 8.00 ÷ 6.40 = 1.25 m/s² Fnet50 − 20 = 30 N Checkm = Fnet ÷ a = 30 ÷ 1.25 = 24 kg (from one trial; the graph uses all five)

Step 4 · Graph in Google Sheets

  1. Column A: Acceleration (m/s²). Column B: Net force (N). Enter your 5 trials.
  2. Select both columns → Insert → Chart → Scatter chart. Make sure acceleration is on the x-axis.
  3. Chart editor → Customize → Series → check Trendline, set Label to Use equation.
  4. Add a title and axis labels with units. Paste the graph into your Lab Doc.
  5. The slope is your crate's mass in kg (because N ÷ (m/s²) = kg).

Part 2: The Elevator Scale

Vertical forces and the "lying" scale. ≈ 30 min

The story

A 55 kg student stands on a bathroom scale in an elevator and rides from the lobby up several floors. A scale doesn't measure weight directly. It measures how hard it has to push up on you: the normal force. Watch what happens to that number.

Procedure

  1. Press ▶ Play and watch the whole 12-second ride once. Check slow motion if it's too fast.
  2. Use the −0.5 s / +0.5 s buttons to stop at exactly 0, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 12 s. Record the velocity at each time in the table in your Lab Doc.
  3. In each interval, pause in the middle (for example, at 3 s) and record the scale reading.
  4. Calculate the acceleration for each interval: a = Δv ÷ Δt (up = +).
  5. Predict the scale reading with ΣFy = ma: FN − mg = ma → FN = m(g + a). Compare it to what the scale showed.

Think about it while you work

  • When is the scale reading bigger than mg? When is it smaller?
  • Does a big velocity change the scale reading, or does only acceleration change it?
Then: draw the two FBDs (section 2B) and answer Part 2 Questions 8–11 in your Lab Doc.
