1. Testable Questions in Science (01.01)

Key Ideas
  • A question is testable when you can investigate it with observations and measurements of the natural world — not with opinions, values, or beliefs.
  • Science can offer an explanation only when that explanation is based on empirical evidence (data you can observe and measure).
  • A good testable question produces data that are measurable, objective, and repeatable — another person running the same test should get similar results.
  • Testable questions explore how the natural world behaves: cause and effect, patterns, and measurable relationships between variables.
  • Not testable by science: questions of ethics ("should we…"), aesthetics ("which is prettiest"), or personal preference ("what is the best…").
🌱 Real-World Connection
"Does adding salt to water make it boil faster?" is testable — you can measure boiling times. "Is salted pasta water tastier?" is not a science question — taste is a preference, and different people will answer differently no matter how carefully you measure. Consumer-product labs, medical trials, and sports-science studies all live or die by whether their question can be turned into repeatable measurements.
Check Your Understanding
  1. Which of the following is a testable scientific question?
    • Is it fair to keep large animals in zoos?
    • Does the color of light change how fast radish seeds sprout?
    • Which painting in the museum is the most beautiful?
    • Should the school day start one hour later?
  2. The results of a well-designed scientific investigation should be:
    • based mostly on the researcher's personal opinion
    • impossible for anyone else to repeat
    • measurable and repeatable
    • different every time, with no pattern
  3. Science can provide an explanation for a question when the explanation is built on:
    • tradition and popular belief
    • observations and evidence from the natural world
    • how strongly people feel about the answer
    • a vote among the scientists in the room
Show answers
1. (b) — light color and sprouting time can both be measured, and the test can be repeated.
2. (c) — measurable and repeatable data are what let other scientists check the results.
3. (b) — explanations in science must rest on evidence from the natural world.

2. Reading a Measurement: Quantity, Magnitude & Direction (01.04)

Key Ideas

Every measurement in physics has parts. Take velocity = 25 m/s west:

  • Physical quantity — what is being measured. Here: velocity. (Other examples: force, acceleration, mass, temperature.)
  • Magnitude — the size: the number together with its unit. Here: 25 m/s.
  • Direction — which way. Here: west.

Scalars vs. vectors

  • Scalar = magnitude only. Examples: distance, speed, mass, time, temperature.
  • Vector = magnitude and direction. Examples: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force.
🚗 Real-World Connection
Your car's odometer reports a scalar — "you have driven 300 miles," no direction. Your GPS reports vectors — "the destination is 12 miles to the northeast," and "head south on Main St." A weather report saying "wind 20 mph from the west" is a vector; saying "it is 72°F" is a scalar.
Check Your Understanding

Use the measurement acceleration = 9.8 m/s² downward for questions 1–3.

  1. What is the physical quantity in this measurement?
  2. What is the magnitude in this measurement?
  3. What is the direction in this measurement?
  4. Which of these is a vector quantity: mass, temperature, velocity, or distance?
  5. Which of these is a scalar quantity: displacement, force, speed, or acceleration?
  6. Explain why "a runner jogged 5 km" describes a scalar, but "a runner jogged 5 km north" describes a vector.
Show answers
1. acceleration    2. 9.8 m/s²    3. downward
4. velocity (it names a direction)    5. speed (magnitude only)
6. "5 km" gives only a magnitude, so it is distance (a scalar). Adding "north" gives a direction, making it displacement (a vector).

3. Frames of Reference & Relative Motion (01.04)

Key Ideas
  • How fast something appears to move depends on your frame of reference — what you are measuring the motion against (the ground, another vehicle, etc.).
  • A speedometer shows speed relative to the ground. It says nothing about direction, so it reports a scalar.
  • Same direction: the relative speed between two objects is the difference of their speeds. The faster object pulls ahead; the slower one appears to drift backward.
  • Opposite directions: the relative speed is the sum of their speeds (they separate or approach quickly).
  • Two objects moving at the same velocity (same speed and direction) appear motionless relative to each other.
🚃 Real-World Connection
Sitting in a stopped train, when the train on the next track rolls forward you briefly feel like you are moving backward — your brain picked the wrong frame of reference. On the highway, passing a car that is only slightly slower than you feels like it is crawling backward past your window, even though it is doing 70 mph relative to the road.
Check Your Understanding
  1. A bus reads 50 mph. It passes a car traveling 45 mph in the same direction. To a passenger looking out of the bus, the car appears to:
    • race forward quickly
    • drift slowly backward
    • stay exactly even with the window
    • move backward at 95 mph
  2. A drone flies 30 mph east directly above a runner going 8 mph east. The drone's speed relative to the runner is:
    • 38 mph east
    • 22 mph east
    • 8 mph east
    • 0 mph
  3. Two cyclists both ride north at exactly 15 mph, side by side. Their velocity relative to each other is:
    • 30 mph north
    • 15 mph north
    • 0 mph — they appear motionless to each other
    • impossible to tell
Show answers
1. (b) — same direction, so relative speed is 50 − 45 = 5 mph; the slower car slips backward slowly.
2. (b) — 30 − 8 = 22 mph east.
3. (c) — equal speed and equal direction means zero relative velocity.

4. Building an Accurate Graph (01.05)

Key Ideas
  • Independent variable = what the experimenter changes on purpose. It goes on the x-axis (horizontal).
  • Dependent variable = what is measured and responds to the change. It goes on the y-axis (vertical).
  • Title format: "Dependent variable vs. Independent variable" — e.g. "Volume vs. Mass."
  • Every axis needs a label and a unit.
  • Scales must be uniform: equal steps in value must be equal distances on the axis (0, 2, 4, 6, 8… — not 0, 2, 4, 5, 7… drawn evenly spaced).

Common graphing errors: axes swapped (independent variable placed on the y-axis); inconsistent/uneven interval spacing; missing units; a line drawn beyond the data that was actually collected.

📈 Real-World Connection
News graphics get called out constantly for uneven or truncated axes that exaggerate a trend. In a lab, if you grow bean plants and record height every day, "day number" is what you chose (x-axis) and "plant height" is what nature decided (y-axis) — the title is "Plant Height vs. Day."
Check Your Understanding

A student changed the mass on a scale and measured the acceleration of a cart for each mass, then graphed the results.

  1. What is the independent variable?
  2. What is the dependent variable?
  3. What should the title of the graph be?
  4. The student put mass on the y-axis and acceleration on the x-axis. What is the error?
  5. The student labeled the y-axis 0, 10, 20, 25, 40, 60 with the tick marks evenly spaced. What is the error?
  6. The student collected data only up to 5 kg but drew the best-fit line out to 12 kg. Why is that a problem?
Show answers
1. mass (the student chose it)    2. acceleration (it was measured / responded)
3. "Acceleration vs. Mass"
4. The axes are swapped — the independent variable (mass) belongs on the x-axis.
5. The scale intervals are not consistent; equal spaces on the axis must represent equal changes in value.
6. The line now shows data that were never collected; you cannot assume the pattern continues past the measured range.

5. Recognizing Relationships from a Graph (01.06)

Key Ideas
  • Linear / direct: a straight line, usually through the origin. As x increases, y increases by the same factor. Double x → double y.
  • Quadratic (nonlinear increasing): an upward-bending curve that gets steeper. y grows much faster than x (often y ∝ x²). Double x → y increases about four times.
  • Inverse: a curve that falls steeply then levels off, approaching but never touching the axes. As x increases, y decreases. Double x → y is cut in half (y ∝ 1/x).
🛠️ Real-World Connection
  • Linear: the stretch of a spring vs. the force pulling it (Hooke's law). Twice the force, twice the stretch.
  • Quadratic: a car's kinetic energy vs. its speed. Going twice as fast means about four times the energy — and roughly four times the braking distance.
  • Inverse: for a fixed trip, travel time vs. driving speed. Drive twice as fast, arrive in half the time.
Check Your Understanding
  1. A graph of "distance stretched vs. hanging weight" is a straight line starting at the origin. What kind of relationship is this?
  2. A graph of "kinetic energy vs. speed" is a curve that starts shallow and bends sharply upward. What kind of relationship is this?
  3. A graph of "electric current vs. resistance" (at constant voltage) is a curve that drops fast and then flattens near both axes. What kind of relationship is this?
  4. In a direct/linear relationship, if one variable is tripled, what happens to the other?
  5. In an inverse relationship, if one variable is tripled, what happens to the other?
Show answers
1. Linear (direct) — straight line through the origin.
2. Quadratic / nonlinear increasing — y grows much faster than x.
3. Inverse — as one goes up the other goes down, curving toward the axes.
4. It is also tripled.
5. It becomes one-third as large.

6. Speed & Average Speed (01.07)

Key Ideas
  • speed = distance ÷ time. Keep units consistent (meters and seconds → m/s).
  • Average speed = total distance ÷ total time, even if the object sped up, slowed down, or stopped along the way.
  • On a position–time graph, the average speed from the start to some time t is the position reached at t divided by t.
  • A flat (horizontal) part of a position–time graph is a period when the object was not moving — but that time still counts in an average-speed calculation.
🚚 Real-World Connection
A road-trip app that says "270 miles in 6 hours, average 45 mph" is doing exactly this calculation — the low average reflects the gas stops and traffic, even though you cruised at 70 mph on the open highway.
Check Your Understanding
  1. A runner covers 150 m in 25 s. What is the runner's speed?
  2. A train travels 2,000 m in 80 s. What is the train's speed?
  3. A skater glides 84 m in 12 s. What is the skater's speed?
  4. Using the graph above, what is the average speed from the start until t = 1 hour?
  5. Using the graph above, what is the average speed from the start until t = 2 hours?
  6. Using the graph above, what is the average speed from the start until t = 4 hours?
Show answers
1. 150 ÷ 25 = 6 m/s
2. 2000 ÷ 80 = 25 m/s
3. 84 ÷ 12 = 7 m/s
4. 50 mi ÷ 1 h = 50 mph
5. 50 mi ÷ 2 h = 25 mph (the car was parked from t = 1 to 2, but that hour still counts)
6. 90 mi ÷ 4 h = 22.5 mph

7. Slope of a Position–Time Graph (01.08)

Key Ideas
  • slope = rise ÷ run = (position₂ − position₁) ÷ (time₂ − time₁)
  • On a position–time graph, the slope is the velocity.
  • Steeper line → faster. Flat line → at rest (velocity = 0). Downward slope → moving back toward the start.
  • Constant speed shows up as a straight line (equal distance covered in each equal time interval).
  • Speeding up → the curve gets steeper over time. Slowing down → the curve flattens over time.
  • In a data table: constant speed means each equal distance step takes the same amount of time. Increasing time per step = slowing down; decreasing time per step = speeding up.
🚇 Real-World Connection
A fitness-tracker "pace" graph is a slope reading: a steep section of the distance–time line is a sprint, a flat section is a water break. Cars roll faster and speed up more on a smooth road than on gravel — the surface changes the shape of the motion graph.
Check Your Understanding — Part A (slope from a curve)

A position–time graph passes through these points: (0 s, 0 m), (1 s, 3 m), (2 s, 5 m), (3 s, 6 m), (4 s, 6.5 m).

  1. Calculate the slope of the segment from t = 1 s to t = 2 s.
  2. Calculate the slope of the segment from t = 2 s to t = 3 s.
  3. Is this object speeding up or slowing down? How do you know?

Part B (segmented graph) — points: (0 h, 0 mi), (1 h, 20 mi), (3 h, 20 mi), (4 h, 60 mi).

  1. Calculate the slope from t = 1 h to t = 3 h. What was the object doing?
  2. Calculate the slope from t = 3 h to t = 4 h.

8. Reading Motion Graphs: At Rest vs. Moving (01.09)

Key Ideas
  • Position–time graph: a sloped line = moving; a horizontal (flat) line = at rest (position is not changing).
  • Speed–time graph: the object is at rest when the line sits on the x-axis (speed = 0); it is moving whenever the line is above the axis.
  • Translating a position–time graph into a speed–time graph:
    • Straight sloped line (constant speed) → speed–time graph is a horizontal line above zero.
    • Horizontal position line (at rest) → speed–time graph is a horizontal line on the x-axis (zero).
    • A downward-sloping position line still means moving, so its speed–time graph is a horizontal line above zero (speed is a magnitude — it has no sign).
Row 3: the position line goes down (returning toward the start) — still moving, so the speed–time graph is a flat line above zero, same as row 2.
🛣️ Real-World Connection
A GPS breadcrumb trail that keeps pinging the same coordinates (flat position graph) means the phone — and probably its owner — is sitting still. A cruise-control drive at a steady 65 mph is the straight-sloped position line and the flat speed line at the same time.
Check Your Understanding
  1. On a speed–time graph, the line runs along the x-axis from t = 4 s to t = 8 s. During that interval, is the object at rest or moving?
  2. On a position–time graph, the line is horizontal from t = 0 s to t = 3 s, then slopes upward. During which interval is the object at rest?
  3. A position–time graph is a straight line from (0 s, 0 m) to (5 s, 25 m). Describe the matching speed–time graph.
  4. A position–time graph is a horizontal line at position 10 m for the whole trip. Describe the matching speed–time graph.
  5. A position–time graph is a straight line from (0 s, 25 m) down to (5 s, 0 m). Describe the matching speed–time graph.
Show answers
1. At rest — speed is zero whenever the line is on the x-axis.
2. From t = 0 s to t = 3 s (the flat part of the position graph).
3. A horizontal line at a constant positive speed of 5 m/s (25 m ÷ 5 s).
4. A horizontal line right on the x-axis — speed = 0 the whole time.
5. A horizontal line at a constant positive speed of 5 m/s. The object is moving back toward the origin, but speed has no direction, so the value is still +5.

9. Velocity & Change in Velocity (01.10)

Key Ideas
  • Velocity is a vector: speed plus direction.
  • Velocity changes if any of these change: the speed, the direction, or both. A car rounding a curve at a steady 30 mph is changing velocity, because its direction is changing.
  • Displacement = straight-line distance from start to finish, with direction. Going out and coming back can give a large distance but a small (or zero) displacement.
  • average velocity = displacement ÷ total time — and the answer must include a direction.
  • Pick one direction as positive (e.g. east +, west −). Add the legs, then attach the direction of the result.
🏃 Real-World Connection
Run one full lap of a 400 m track and your distance is 400 m, but your displacement is 0 — you finished where you started, so your average velocity for the lap is 0 m/s even though your average speed was not. A merry-go-round horse moves at constant speed but its velocity is changing every instant because its direction keeps turning.
Check Your Understanding
  1. Which of the following shows a change in velocity?
    • a car cruising straight down a flat highway at a steady 55 mph
    • a race car going around a circular track at a steady 120 mph
    • a book resting on a shelf
    • a hockey puck sliding in a straight line at constant speed
  2. Give one more example of your own that shows a change in velocity, and say what is changing (speed, direction, or both).
  3. Maria walks 60.0 m north in 120.0 s, then 20.0 m south in 40.0 s. What is her average velocity for the trip?
  4. Devon walks 80.0 m east in 100.0 s, then 80.0 m west in 100.0 s. What is his average velocity for the trip?
  5. Sam jogs 100.0 m west in 50.0 s, then 40.0 m east in 20.0 s. What is his average velocity for the trip? How does it compare to his average speed?
Show answers
1. (b) — the speed is constant but the direction keeps changing, so the velocity changes.
2. Sample: a ball dropped from your hand speeds up as it falls (speed changing); a car merging onto a curved on-ramp while accelerating (both changing).
3. Displacement = 60 m north − 20 m south = 40 m north. Total time = 160 s. Average velocity = 40 ÷ 160 = 0.25 m/s north.
4. Displacement = 80 − 80 = 0 m. Average velocity = 0 ÷ 200 = 0 m/s (he ended where he started).
5. Displacement = 100 W − 40 = 60 m west. Total time = 70 s. Average velocity = 60 ÷ 70 ≈ 0.86 m/s west. Average speed = total distance 140 m ÷ 70 s = 2.0 m/s — larger, because speed counts every meter walked, not just the net change in position.

Key Vocabulary

Testable question
A question that can be investigated with measurements and observations of the natural world, giving repeatable data.
Empirical evidence
Information gained from observation, measurement, or experiment — the basis of every scientific explanation.
Physical quantity
The property being measured (velocity, force, acceleration, mass, …).
Magnitude
The size of a measurement: its number together with its unit.
Scalar
A quantity with magnitude only (distance, speed, mass, time, temperature).
Vector
A quantity with magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force).
Frame of reference
The object or background against which motion is measured.
Independent variable
The variable the experimenter changes on purpose; plotted on the x-axis.
Dependent variable
The variable that responds and is measured; plotted on the y-axis.
Linear (direct) relationship
A straight-line graph; the two variables change by the same factor.
Inverse relationship
As one variable increases, the other decreases; the graph is a curve approaching both axes.
Speed
Distance divided by time; a scalar.
Velocity
Speed in a stated direction; a vector.
Displacement
The change in position measured in a straight line from start to finish, with direction.
Slope (of a position–time graph)
Rise over run; equal to the object's velocity. Zero slope means the object is at rest.
