Top Mistakes — Don't Be That Student

❌ Rearranging
Don't leave a fraction in the denominator. Clear fractions first, then isolate.
❌ Negative acceleration
Negative acceleration isn't always "bad" — it just means the acceleration points opposite to the direction you chose as positive.
❌ Graphs
On a v-t graph, slope = acceleration, NOT speed. On an a-t graph, the AREA under the curve = change in velocity.
❌ Newton's 3rd Law
Action-reaction pairs act on DIFFERENT objects — that's why they don't cancel. If forces are on the same object, they can balance.
❌ Free fall
You must NEGLECT air resistance to use g = 9.8 m/s². Air resistance changes everything.
❌ Show your work
No work shown = no partial credit. Always: list known values → write equation → substitute → solve → units.
Solving & Rearranging Equations
The exam will give you a formula and ask you to solve for a different variable. Here's the method.
🔢

The 5-Step Method

1
Write down the original equation exactly as given.
2
Identify the variable you want to isolate.
3
If there's a fraction, multiply both sides to clear it first.
4
Use inverse operations (division undoes multiplication, subtraction undoes addition) to get your variable alone.
5
Check: no fractions left in the denominator. Units make sense.
📐

Exam Examples — Step by Step

Example 1: Solve V = IR for R

1
V = IR
2
R is multiplied by I. Divide both sides by I.
3
V/I = IR/I → the I's cancel on the right
R = V / I ✓

Example 2: Solve v = d/t for t

1
v = d/t — there's a fraction. Clear it first.
2
Multiply both sides by t: v·t = d
3
Now t is multiplied by v. Divide both sides by v.
t = d / v ✓

Example 3: Solve F = ma for m

1
F = ma. No fraction. m is multiplied by a.
2
Divide both sides by a: F/a = ma/a
m = F / a ✓
Students sometimes try to solve V = IR for R and write R = VI. That's wrong — if R was multiplied by I, you divide by I, not multiply. Always ask: what operation connects the variable to what's next to it? Then do the opposite.
✏️

Practice — Try Before You Check

Solve F = ma for a
a = F / m (divide both sides by m)
Solve d = ½gt² for t
Multiply both sides by 2: 2d = gt². Then divide by g: t² = 2d/g. Then take the square root: t = √(2d/g)
Solve V = IR for I
I = V / R (divide both sides by R)
Solve a = (vf − vo)/t for vf
Multiply both sides by t: at = vf − vo. Then add vo: vf = at + vo
Kinematics — Motion Calculations
These are the train problems, the car problems, the "how far did it travel" problems. One formula does most of the work.
🚂

The Key Equation

d = vot + ½at²
d = displacement (meters) · v₀ = initial velocity (m/s) · a = acceleration (m/s²) · t = time (s)
When acceleration is negative — the ½at² part subtracts from the distance. That's how a train slowing down travels less than you'd expect from the initial velocity alone.
📐

Worked Example — Train Problem

EXAM TYPE

"A train is slowing down with an average acceleration of −6.0 m/s². If its initial velocity is 60.0 m/s, how far does it travel in 5.0 seconds?"

1
Identify your data: v₀ = 60.0 m/s · a = −6.0 m/s² · t = 5.0 s · d = ?
2
Write the equation: d = v₀t + ½at²
3
Substitute: d = (60.0)(5.0) + ½(−6.0)(5.0)²
4
d = 300 + ½(−6.0)(25) = 300 + (−75) = 300 − 75
d = 225 m ≈ 230 m ✓
The train is slowing down, so acceleration points opposite to its motion. We call the direction of motion positive, so acceleration is negative. This is deceleration — the train is still moving forward, just getting slower.
✏️

Try It — Second Train Problem

EXAM TYPE

"A train slows down at −4.0 m/s². Initial velocity is 50.0 m/s. How far does it travel in 8.0 s?"

Data: v₀=50, a=−4.0, t=8.0
d = (50)(8) + ½(−4)(64) = 400 + (−128) = 272 m ≈ 270 m
📋

Three Types of Acceleration to Know

🚀
Speeding Up
v and a point same direction. a is positive (in the direction of motion).
🛑
Slowing Down
a points opposite to v. a is negative. Object is decelerating.
↩️
Changing Direction
Speed may stay constant, but velocity changes. Still acceleration.
Which of these is acceleration? — On the exam: changing direction is acceleration. Changing mass is NOT. Changing position alone is NOT (that's just motion).
Reading Motion Graphs
The exam has both position-time and acceleration-time graphs. The key: know what slope and area mean on each type.
📈

Cheat Sheet — What Each Graph Tells You

Graph TypeSlope means…Flat line means…Area means…
Position vs. Time Velocity Object is not moving N/A
Velocity vs. Time Acceleration Constant velocity (no acceleration) Displacement
Acceleration vs. Time Jerk (not tested) Constant acceleration Change in velocity (Δv)
Exam tip: "The slope of a velocity-time graph is ____" → Answer: acceleration. That question is basically free points.
📐

Position-Time Graph — Reading Change in Position

EXAM TYPE

Segments:

  • Seg 1 (0→1 hr): 0→40 mi = rising line = moving
  • Seg 2 (1→2 hr): 40→40 mi = flat line = stopped
  • Seg 3 (2→3 hr): 40→60 mi = change in position = 60 − 40 = 20 mi
  • Seg 4 (3→4 hr): 60→75 mi = rising, but slower slope
  • Seg 5 (4→5 hr): 75→75 mi = flat = stopped again
"What is the change in position in the third hour?"
Read from x=2 to x=3: position goes from 40 to 60. Answer: 20 miles.
📐

Acceleration-Time Graph — Finding Δv from Area

EXAM TYPE

"Calculate the change in velocity from 20 to 30 seconds"

1
Find the section from t=20 to t=30s on the graph
2
The acceleration is 6 m/s² (constant in that region)
3
Δv = area = height × width = 6 m/s² × 10 s
Δv = 60 m/s ✓
Free Fall & Gravity
Objects in free fall accelerate at g = 9.8 m/s² — but only when you ignore air resistance.
🌍

Free Fall Essentials

What must you neglect?
Air resistance — without it, all objects fall at exactly the same rate regardless of mass.
g = 9.8 m/s²
Every second, velocity increases by 9.8 m/s. This is a constant near Earth's surface.
Gravity & mass
Gravitational force INCREASES as mass increases. Decreases as distance between objects increases.
d = ½gt²     vf = gt
Use these when dropped from rest (v₀ = 0)
🏢

Willis Tower Problem — Worked Example

EXAM TYPE

"The Willis Tower is 442 m high. A rock is dropped from the top. Neglecting air resistance, how long does it take to reach the ground?"

1
Data: d = 442 m · g = 9.8 m/s² · t = ?
2
Equation: d = ½gt²
3
442 = ½(9.8)t² → 442 = 4.9t²
4
t² = 442/4.9 = 90.2
5
t = √90.2 = 9.50 s
t = 9.50 s ✓
👓

Martha's Glasses — Full Show-Your-Work Example

EXAM TYPE

"Martha's glasses fall from a second-floor window and hit the ground in 2.41 seconds. Find (A) height of window, and (B) impact velocity."

Part A — Height

1
Data: t = 2.41 s · g = 9.8 m/s² · d = ?
2
d = ½gt² = ½(9.8)(2.41)² = ½(9.8)(5.808)
d ≈ 28.5 m

Part B — Impact Velocity

1
vf = gt = (9.8)(2.41)
vf ≈ 23.6 m/s
Newton's Three Laws
Know each law, what it predicts, and what happens when forces are balanced vs. unbalanced.
1

First Law — Law of Inertia

"An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion — unless acted on by an unbalanced force."
Inertia
Resistance to a change in motion. Determined by mass. More mass = more inertia = harder to start, stop, or change direction.
Key exam point
Moving objects do NOT need a force to keep moving. They need a force to change their motion. This is what most students get wrong.
Which statement describes Newton's 1st Law?
→ "Describes what an object in motion will do until acted on by an unbalanced force."
(NOT about mass changing, NOT about inertia being reduced)
2

Second Law — F = ma

"The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force, and inversely proportional to its mass."
F = ma  →  a = F/m  →  m = F/a
Directly proportional
Force ↑ → Acceleration ↑ (double the force = double the acceleration, same mass)
Inversely proportional
Mass ↑ → Acceleration ↓ (double the mass = half the acceleration, same force)

David's Bike — Exam Example

"David's bike accelerates from rest to 45.0 m/s in 6.00 s. Combined mass = 225.43 kg. Find the unbalanced force."
1
First find a: a = (vf − vo)/t = (45.0 − 0)/6.00 = 7.50 m/s²
2
Then find F: F = ma = (225.43)(7.50)
F ≈ 1,691 N ✓
3

Third Law — Action-Reaction

"For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force."
Key fact: Different objects
Action and reaction forces always act on different objects. That's why they don't cancel.
True for all objects
It's true for ALL objects interacting — not just objects at rest. Always, always.
Exam statement: "Whenever an object exerts a force on another, the second object exerts a force of the same magnitude in the opposite direction."
→ True for all objects interacting (not just at rest, and they don't cancel)
Examples on the exam:
  • Your foot pushes the ground backward → ground pushes your foot forward
  • Earth pulls rock down → rock pulls Earth up (same magnitude, Earth just barely moves)
  • Rocket pushes gas backward → gas pushes rocket forward
Free Body Diagrams
You won't be asked to draw FBDs on this exam — but reading and interpreting them is fair game, and they're the foundation for every force calculation you'll do.
📌

FBD Rules — Always

1
One object only. Draw ONLY forces acting ON that object — not forces it exerts.
2
All arrows start at the dot (the object) and point away in the direction of each force.
3
Arrow length = force size. Equal forces get equal-length arrows.
4
Label every arrow with its symbol (Fg, FN, Fa, Ff, FT).
5
No motion arrows. Velocity and acceleration are NOT forces — don't put them on the diagram.
🔤

Common FBD Mistakes on Exams

❌
Drawing a "force of motion" arrow pointing in the direction the object is moving — if nothing is pushing it that way, there's NO arrow.
❌
Forgetting the normal force when the object is on a surface. If it's touching something solid, FN is there.
❌
Drawing the force the object exerts on something else. FBDs show forces ON the object only.
❌
Thinking FN and Fg are a Newton's 3rd Law pair — they're not. Both act on the SAME object (the box). Third-law pairs are on different objects.
