Review: Vectors

Forces are vectors, so this is where everything starts.

Scalar

Size only

A scalar has a magnitude (a size, with units) and nothing else.

5 kg · 20 s · 30 °C · 12 m/s (speed)

Vector

Size + direction

A vector has a magnitude and a direction. Change either one and it's a different vector.

12 m/s east · 50 N down · 3 m/s² up

Try it: scalar or vector?

Click each quantity to cycle through Scalar and Vector, then press Check.

Directions become + and − signs

This week every vector points horizontally or vertically. That means we can describe direction with a simple sign:

AxisPositive (+)Negative (−)
x (horizontal)right →left ←
y (vertical)up ↑down ↓

So "50 N to the left" is written Fx = −50 N, and "20 N up" is Fy = +20 N.

Adding vectors on the same line

To add vectors, place them tip-to-tail: start the second arrow where the first one ends. The resultant (R) goes from the very first tail to the very last tip. When the vectors lie on the same line, that's just adding signed numbers:

same direction → add sizes  ·  opposite directions → subtract, and keep the sign of the bigger one

The Newton: the Unit of Force

How we measure pushes and pulls.

1 newton (N) is the force that makes a 1 kg mass accelerate at 1 m/s².

1 N = 1 kg · m/s²

It comes straight from Newton's second law, F = ma. Plug in m = 1 kg and a = 1 m/s² and you get 1 N.

Weight is a force, measured in newtons

Mass (kg) is how much matter something has. It's the same everywhere. Weight (N) is the force of gravity pulling on that mass. On Earth:

Fg = m · g    with   g = 9.8 m/s²

Say "a 2 kg book has a mass of 2 kg and a weight of 19.6 N." Don't mix them up.

Review: Newton's Three Laws

The rules that connect forces to motion.

1st Law · Inertia

No net force → no change in motion

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving at a constant velocity, unless a net force acts on it. Objects don't need a force to keep moving. They need a force to change their motion.

2nd Law

Net force causes acceleration

ΣF = m · a   →   a = ΣF ÷ m

ΣF ("sigma F") means the net force: all the forces added together as vectors. A bigger net force means more acceleration. A bigger mass means less. The acceleration always points the same direction as the net force.

3rd Law

Forces come in pairs

If object A pushes on object B, then B pushes back on A with a force that is equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other out on one FBD.

Drawing Free Body Diagrams

A picture of every force acting on one object.

The rules

  1. One object only. Draw only forces acting on that object, never forces it exerts on something else.
  2. Shrink it to a dot. Every arrow starts at the dot and points away from it.
  3. Arrow length shows size. Bigger force → longer arrow. Equal forces → equal arrows.
  4. Label every arrow with its symbol (Fg, FN…) and its value if you know it.
  5. No motion arrows. Velocity and acceleration are not forces, so they don't go on an FBD.

The force catalog

5

Adding Forces: Net Force

Turn an FBD into an equation.

Handle each direction separately

Horizontal forces only affect horizontal motion, and vertical forces only affect vertical motion. So add each axis on its own:

ΣFx = (forces right) − (forces left)
ΣFy = (forces up) − (forces down)

Then apply Newton's second law along each axis: ΣFx = m·ax and ΣFy = m·ay.

Balanced (ΣF = 0)
a = 0. The object is at rest or moving at constant velocity. Both count!
Unbalanced (ΣF ≠ 0)
The object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

The 4-step method (use it on every problem)

  1. Draw the FBD. Every force, labeled.
  2. Choose + directions (usually right and up).
  3. Write ΣF = ma for each axis that matters.
  4. Substitute and solve. Check that units and signs make sense.

Worked Examples

Six situations, each solved with the 4-step method. Press ▶ to watch the motion.

Fg weightFN normalFa appliedFf frictionFT tensionFair airΣF net (dashed)
Example 1 · At rest

A 2.0 kg book resting on a table

Find the normal force.

ForcesWeight down, normal force up. Nothing horizontal. WeightFg = mg = (2.0)(9.8) = 19.6 N ΣFy = mayFN − Fg = (2.0)(0)  →  FN = 19.6 N up TakeawayAt rest on a flat surface, with nothing else pushing up or down, the normal force equals the weight.
Example 2 · Hanging

A 3.0 kg lamp hanging from a cord

Find the tension in the cord.

ForcesWeight down, tension up (the cord pulls along itself, away from the lamp). WeightFg = (3.0)(9.8) = 29.4 N ΣFy = mayFT − Fg = 0  →  FT = 29.4 N up TakeawayNo surface touches the lamp, so there's no normal force. The cord does the holding.
Example 3 · Constant velocity

Pushing a 20 kg crate at a steady speed

Friction on the crate is 60 N. How hard must you push to keep it moving at a constant velocity?

The dots (every 0.5 s) are evenly spaced, so the velocity is constant.
ForcesFg and FN (vertical); push Fa right, friction Ff left. VerticalFN = Fg = (20)(9.8) = 196 N ΣFx = maxFa − 60 = (20)(0)  →  Fa = 60 N TakeawayConstant velocity means a = 0. The push only has to match friction, not beat it.
Example 4 · Accelerating

Pushing the same crate harder

Now you push the 20 kg crate with 100 N. Friction is still 60 N. Find the acceleration.

The dots spread apart, so the crate is speeding up.
ΣFx100 − 60 = 40 N right (the dashed net force arrow) a = ΣF ÷ ma = 40 ÷ 20 = 2.0 m/s² right VerticalStill balanced: FN = Fg = 196 N TakeawayOnly the leftover (net) force accelerates the crate, not the whole 100 N.
Example 5 · Vertical acceleration

A 60 kg student on a scale in an elevator

The elevator accelerates upward at 1.5 m/s². What does the scale read?

A scale reads the normal force it pushes up with.
ForcesWeight down, scale's normal force up. WeightFg = (60)(9.8) = 588 N ΣFy = mayFN − 588 = (60)(+1.5) = 90 SolveFN = 678 N. The scale reads more than the student's weight! TakeawayTo accelerate upward, the upward force must beat the weight. That's why you feel "heavier" when an elevator starts going up.
Example 6 · Air resistance

A 70 kg skydiver

Compare two moments: a few seconds after jumping, when air resistance has grown to 300 N, and later at terminal velocity. (Right when she jumps, air resistance is almost zero, because she isn't moving fast yet.)

WeightFg = (70)(9.8) = 686 N down ΣFy300 − 686 = −386 N (the minus sign means down) a = ΣF ÷ m−386 ÷ 70 = −5.5 m/s² What the sign meansHere the minus sign means the acceleration points down. She is also moving down, so acceleration and velocity point the same way, and she speeds up. (Acceleration opposite to the velocity would slow her down.)
Why it changesAir resistance grows as she falls faster, until it equals her weight. ΣFy686 − 686 = 0  →  a = 0 TakeawayShe's falling fast but at a constant velocity. That's terminal velocity, Newton's 1st law in action.
