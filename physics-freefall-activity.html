PART 1 — YOUR PREDICTIONS

Before we explain anything — what do you think?

In the video, Brian Cox drops a bowling ball and a feather at the same time inside NASA's massive vacuum chamber. He does it twice — once with air inside, once with all air pumped out. Make your predictions first, then we'll reveal the answers.

🔮 PREDICTION 1 — WITH AIR IN THE CHAMBER
Which hits the ground first when the chamber is full of air?

The bowling ball hits first. With air present, air resistance slows the feather dramatically — its large surface area and tiny mass mean it practically floats down. The bowling ball is so heavy that the same air drag barely matters. This matches everyday experience. ✓

🔮 PREDICTION 2 — WITH ALL AIR PUMPED OUT (VACUUM)
Now all air is removed. Which hits first?

They hit at exactly the same time. Without air resistance, the only force acting is gravity — and gravity pulls all objects toward Earth at the same rate, regardless of mass. The feather and the bowling ball are identical in free fall. That's the central discovery of this unit.

💨

Air Resistance

Air particles collide with falling objects and push back against their motion. A feather has a huge surface area relative to its tiny mass — air resistance dominates. A bowling ball is so massive that the same air drag is nearly meaningless.

🌍

Gravity

Gravity pulls every object toward Earth's center at the same rate: g = 9.8 m/s². It doesn't care about mass. The feather and bowling ball feel gravity equally — it's only air resistance that creates the difference we normally see.

🏛️

Galileo's Discovery

Around 1590, Galileo Galilei claimed all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass. People thought he was wrong — because in real life, heavy things DO fall faster (thanks to air). He had to think beyond what he could see. He was right.

🚀

Free Fall

"Free fall" means gravity is the only force acting — no air, no friction, nothing else. Every object in free fall accelerates at 9.8 m/s² downward. After 1 second: 9.8 m/s fast. After 2 seconds: 19.6 m/s. It keeps growing — that's acceleration.

NASA confirmed it on the Moon

Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the airless surface of the Moon in 1971. They hit at exactly the same time — broadcast live on television. Galileo's 400-year-old prediction, confirmed in front of the whole world.

PART 3 — CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Five questions — discuss as a class before clicking

Don't rush these. Talk through each one together before selecting an answer. The explanation that appears after each question is part of the activity.

QUESTION 1 OF 5
In the video with air, why does the feather fall so much slower than the bowling ball?
Think about what force — other than gravity — is acting on both objects.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
When all the air is pumped out, what changes — and why?
What was slowing the feather before? What happens when that's removed?
QUESTION 3 OF 5
What does g = 9.8 m/s² actually mean?
Look at the units carefully — m/s² means meters per second, per second.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
⚽ A soccer ball and a penny are dropped from the top of an empty stadium at the same moment — no wind, no air resistance. Which hits the ground first?
Apply Galileo's discovery to a real-world situation.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
Why did most people in Galileo's time think he was wrong — even though he wasn't?
What do people observe in everyday life that would lead them to the wrong conclusion?
The big idea you just discovered

Gravity accelerates all objects equally at g = 9.8 m/s². Air resistance creates the differences we see in everyday life. Remove the air, and every falling object — feather, bowling ball, soccer ball, penny — falls in perfect unison. The formulas we'll learn next all flow from this single fact.
