The big picture

In earlier units you learned how to describe motion with words, graphs and numbers. This unit explains why things move the way they do. In the 1600s, Isaac Newton wrote three laws that predict how any object, whether a planet, a car or a soccer ball, responds to the forces acting on it.

Law 1 · Law of inertia

Objects keep doing what they're doing

…unless an unbalanced force acts on them.

ΣF = 0 → a = 0
Law 2 · Law of acceleration

Net force causes acceleration

More force means more acceleration. More mass means less acceleration.

a = Fnet / m
Law 3 · Action–reaction

Forces come in pairs

If A pushes on B, then B pushes back on A with an equal and opposite force.

FA on B = −FB on A

  • Explain inertia and how it depends on mass.
  • Tell balanced forces from unbalanced forces and find the net force.
  • Draw and read free-body diagrams.
  • Use Fnet = m·a to find acceleration, force or mass.
  • Explain free fall, air resistance and terminal velocity.
  • Identify action–reaction force pairs and explain why they don't cancel.
0 Foundation

What is a force?

A force is a push or a pull on an object that comes from its interaction with another object. When the interaction stops, the force stops. A force isn't something an object "has" or "carries." It only exists while two things are interacting.

Force is a vector, which means it has both a size (magnitude) and a direction. We draw forces as arrows: the length shows how strong the force is, and the arrowhead shows which way it acts. The SI unit of force is the newton (N). One newton is about the weight of a small apple. A size-5 soccer ball weighs about 4.2 N.

🤝 Contact forces

The objects are touching.

Applied, friction, normal, tension, air resistance, spring

📡 Action-at-a-distance forces

The objects pull or push without touching.

Gravitational, electric, magnetic. Even mid-header with both feet off the ground, Earth is still pulling you down.

⚠️ Mass ≠ weight

Mass (kg) is how much matter is in an object, and it's the same everywhere. Weight (N) is the force of gravity on that mass. On the Moon your mass stays the same, but you'd weigh only about 1/6 as much.

1 Newton's First Law

The law of inertia

Newton's first law An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction, unless acted on by an unbalanced force.

The first law has two parts:

  1. At rest → stays at rest. A ball on the penalty spot won't move until something (your boot) applies an unbalanced force.
  2. In motion → stays in motion in a straight line at constant speed. A pass would roll forever if nothing slowed it. On Earth, the unbalanced force that eventually stops it is usually friction.

Put simply: objects tend to "keep on doing what they're doing."

Inertia and mass

Inertia is an object's resistance to a change in its motion. It describes how much an object "wants" to keep doing what it's already doing.

  • The greater the mass, the greater the inertia, and the more force you need to start it, stop it, or change its direction.
  • Since an object's state of motion is its velocity (speed + direction), inertia is really resistance to changes in velocity. In other words, it's resistance to acceleration.

📜 A bit of history: Galileo's ramps

For about 2,000 years people believed the "natural" state of objects was rest, and that you needed a force to keep something moving. Galileo rolled balls down one ramp and up another. The smoother the ramps, the closer the ball came to its starting height. He reasoned that with zero friction, a ball rolling onto a flat surface would never stop. Friction, not some natural tendency, is what stops moving objects. Newton built his first law on this idea.

Inertia in everyday life

🚗 The sharp turn and the sudden stop

When a car turns sharply, your body feels pulled toward the outside. Really, it's trying to keep going straight. When a car stops suddenly, your body keeps moving forward. That's why seatbelts matter: the belt supplies the unbalanced force that stops you.

🍅 The ketchup bottle

Thrust the upside-down bottle downward fast and stop it abruptly. The bottle stops, but the ketchup keeps moving downward and out.

🏂 Snowboard catches ice

The board and feet stop suddenly, but the rider's upper body keeps moving forward, often face-first into the snow.

🥤 The full water glass

Start walking: the water stays behind and spills backward. Turn: the water keeps going straight and spills to the side. Stop: the water keeps going and spills forward.

⚽ Coach's corner: inertia on the pitch

  • Cutting and changing direction: when a winger plants and cuts, their body "wants" to keep going straight. That's why you drop your hips, lean into the cut, and rely on cleat grip (friction) to supply the unbalanced force that turns you.
  • Strength on the ball: a heavier centre-back has more inertia, so they're harder to knock off the ball. They also take more force to accelerate and turn, which is one reason smaller, quicker players can beat them on the dribble.
  • Getting clipped: when a defender catches your trailing foot, your feet stop but your upper body keeps moving forward, just like the snowboarder.
  • Pitch conditions: on a slick, wet surface a ground pass skips and keeps its speed much longer (less friction). On long, dry grass it dies quickly. Try it in the lab below.

🔑 Key takeaways: First Law

  • Balanced forces → the object stays at rest or keeps a constant velocity (same speed, same direction).
  • Unbalanced force → the object accelerates (speeds up, slows down, or changes direction).
  • More mass = more inertia = harder to change its motion.
  • A moving object does not need a force to keep moving. It needs a force to change its motion.
1 Forces in balance

Balanced forces, unbalanced forces & net force

More than one force can act on an object at the same time. The vector sum of all the forces acting on one object is the net force (Fnet or ΣF).

Balanced forces (equilibrium)

Fnet = 0. The object's motion does not change. It stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant velocity.

Unbalanced forces

Fnet ≠ 0. The object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

Adding forces is (mostly) arithmetic

⚽ Coach's corner: shoulder-to-shoulder

Two players chasing a through-ball lean into each other. If each pushes sideways with the same force, the sideways forces are balanced and neither gets knocked off their line. If one pushes harder, the sideways forces are unbalanced, and the weaker player accelerates sideways and off the ball. That's why building core and lower-body strength matters for winning duels.

Drawing free-body diagrams (FBDs)

A free-body diagram shows all the forces acting on one object. Physicists use them to work out the net force.

  1. Draw the object as a box (or dot) and focus on only that object.
  2. Identify every force acting on it. Ask: what's touching it, and is gravity acting? (Always yes on Earth.)
  3. Draw each force as an arrow pointing away from the center in the direction it acts.
  4. Make arrow lengths show relative sizes. Balanced pairs should have equal lengths.
  5. Label every arrow (Fgrav, Fnorm…). Only draw forces that actually exist; there's no fixed number.

Determining the net force from an FBD

Add up the vertical forces (up vs. down) and the horizontal forces (left vs. right) separately. If both sets cancel, Fnet = 0. If either set doesn't cancel, there's an unbalanced force.

Example: A sprinting player has Fgrav = 700 N down, Fnorm = 700 N up, a forward push from the ground (friction on the cleats) of 300 N, and air resistance of 20 N backward.

Vertical: 700 N up − 700 N down = 0 (balanced).
Horizontal: 300 N forward − 20 N backward = 280 N forward.

Fnet = 280 N forward, so the player is accelerating forward.

2 Newton's Second Law

Force, mass & acceleration

The first law tells us what happens when forces are balanced: no acceleration. The second law tells us exactly how much an object accelerates when the forces are unbalanced.

Newton's second law The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, is in the same direction as the net force, and is inversely proportional to the object's mass.
a = Fnet / m   ⟺   Fnet = m · a a in m/s² · Fnet in newtons (N) · m in kilograms (kg) · 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

Vocabulary you need

Acceleration
The rate at which velocity changes with time. The change can be in speed, direction, or both. Acceleration is a change in motion caused by an unbalanced force.
Mass
The quantity of matter ("stuff") in an object. To find acceleration, divide the net force by the mass.
Directly proportional
As one quantity increases, the other increases too, and when one decreases the other decreases. a ∝ F: double the net force, double the acceleration.
Inversely proportional
As one quantity increases, the other decreases proportionally, and vice versa. a ∝ 1/m: double the mass, half the acceleration.
Force
A push or a pull. Any force can cause an object to move or accelerate.
Net force
The resultant (vector sum) of all the forces acting on an object. The key word is "net."

Worked examples

① Finding acceleration

A 0.43 kg ball is struck with an average force of 1,200 N. What is its acceleration?

a = Fnet / m = 1,200 N / 0.43 kg ≈ 2,800 m/s²

That's about 285 times the acceleration of gravity, but it only lasts about 0.01 s.

② Finding a force

A 0.43 kg ground pass slows down at 2.0 m/s² on dry grass. How big is the friction force?

Fnet = m·a = 0.43 kg × 2.0 m/s² = 0.86 N (backward)

Friction is the only unbalanced force, so it is the net force.

③ Sprint start

A 65 kg forward explodes off the mark. The ground pushes them forward with 390 N, and air resistance is 0 at the start. Acceleration?

a = 390 N / 65 kg = 6.0 m/s² forward

④ Multi-force problem

A player drags a sled with a 50 N pull to the right. Friction on the sled is 10 N. The sled weighs 80 N. Find Fnorm, m, Fnet and a.

Fnorm = 80 N (balances weight)
m = Fgrav/g = 80/9.8 = 8.16 kg
Fnet = 50 − 10 = 40 N right
a = 40/8.16 = 4.9 m/s² right

🧠 Challenge: two connected objects ("double trouble")

A player sprints while pulling two sleds in a line: a 20 kg sled tied to the harness, and a 10 kg sled tied behind it. The harness pulls with 90 N. Ignore friction. What is the acceleration, and what is the tension in the rope between the sleds?

Step 1, system analysis (treat both sleds as one object): m = 20 + 10 = 30 kg, so a = 90 N / 30 kg = 3.0 m/s².

Step 2, individual analysis (just the back sled): the only horizontal force on it is the rope tension, so Ftens = m·a = 10 kg × 3.0 m/s² = 30 N.

⚠️ The BIG misconception: "force causes motion"

Many people believe that a moving object must have a force pushing it in the direction it's moving. False! Force causes acceleration (a change in motion), not motion itself.

After your boot leaves the ball, there is no "force of the kick" carrying it toward goal. The ball keeps moving because of inertia. The only forces on it in flight are gravity (pulling it down) and air resistance (opposing its motion). Look at "Long ball in flight" in Lab 3.

🔑 Key takeaways: Second Law

  • Acceleration always points in the direction of the net force, which isn't necessarily the direction of motion. (A slowing pass moves forward but accelerates backward.)
  • Use the net force, not just one force, in F = m·a.
  • a ∝ Fnet and a ∝ 1/m.
2 Applying the Second Law

Free fall, air resistance & terminal velocity

Free fall means gravity is the only force acting. In free fall all objects accelerate at the same rate, g = 9.8 m/s², no matter their mass. Why? A heavier object has a bigger gravitational force, but it also has more mass (inertia) to accelerate. The two effects cancel exactly:

a = Fgrav / m = (m·g) / m = g A 1,000 kg baby elephant and a 1 kg bird both fall at 9.8 m/s² without air.

With air resistance, things change. Air resistance increases as speed increases. As a falling object speeds up:

  1. At first Fair is small, so Fnet is almost all gravity and a ≈ g.
  2. As speed rises, Fair grows, Fnet shrinks, and acceleration decreases. The object is still speeding up, just more slowly.
  3. Eventually Fair = Fgrav, so Fnet = 0 and a = 0. The object falls at a constant terminal velocity.

Heavier objects (for the same size and shape) need more air resistance to balance their weight, so they reach a higher terminal velocity. That's why a soccer ball drops much faster than a beach ball of a similar size.

3 Newton's Third Law

Equal and opposite interactions

A force isn't a thing in itself. It comes from an interaction between two objects. Forces always come in pairs, one action and one reaction, and neither force exists without the other.

Newton's third law For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
Put more precisely: if object A exerts a force on object B, then object B exerts an equal and opposite force on object A.

⚠️ "If the forces are equal and opposite, why don't they cancel?"

Equal and opposite forces only cancel when they act on the same object. An action–reaction pair always acts on two different objects: one force on the ball, one on your head. Each object responds to the force on it.

Earth, the Moon and a falling apple

Earth (mass 5.98 × 1024 kg) pulls on the Moon (7.35 × 1022 kg) to keep it in orbit. Does the Moon pull back on Earth with a smaller force? Most students say yes, but the third law says the forces are exactly equal and opposite.

The same goes for an apple falling from a tree. Earth pulls the apple down, and the apple pulls Earth up with the same force. So why does the apple fall and not Earth? Combine with the second law: a = F/m. Same force, but Earth's mass is enormous, so its acceleration is microscopically small. The apple's mass is tiny, so its acceleration is noticeable (9.8 m/s²).

Action–reaction pairs on the pitch

🏃 Sprinting

Your cleats push the ground backward, so the ground pushes your cleats forward. That forward push from the ground is what accelerates you. On a wet, slippery pitch you can't push back as hard (less friction), so you can't accelerate as quickly. That's why cleat choice matters.

🦘 Jumping for a header

You push down on the ground, and the ground pushes up on you. The harder you drive into the ground, the bigger the upward push and the higher you rise.

🦶 Striking the ball

Boot pushes ball forward, and ball pushes boot backward. You feel that reaction as the "thud" through your foot. Kick a medicine ball and that reaction force could injure you.

🧤 The save

The keeper's gloves push the ball backward to stop it, and the ball pushes the gloves forward. Keepers "give" with their hands to make the stop last longer, which lowers the force.

How to identify a force pair

Name the two interacting objects and write a sentence for each force. The nouns switch places and the direction flips:

Action: Fist hits wall.  →  Reaction: Wall hits fist.
Action: Boot pushes ball forward.  →  Reaction: Ball pushes boot backward.

⚠️ Trap: are Fnorm and Fgrav an action–reaction pair?

For a ball resting on the grass, Fnorm (up) and Fgrav (down) are equal and opposite, but they are not a third-law pair. Both act on the same object (the ball), so they're a balanced pair (first law). The real third-law partners are:

  • Earth pulls ball down ↔ ball pulls Earth up
  • Ground pushes ball up ↔ ball pushes ground down

🔑 Key takeaways: Third Law

  • Forces come from interactions and always come in pairs.
  • The pair is always equal in size, opposite in direction, and acts on two different objects.
  • Equal forces don't mean equal effects. The object with less mass accelerates more (2nd law).
